CASE FILE: COUNTERARGUMENTS

Let's address the case against this.

A case that only makes its own argument isn't a serious one. Here are the strongest points in Ryan Day's favor, and our honest response to each.

Q. He just won a national championship in 2024. How can you fire a coach who won it all?

This is the single strongest argument against this campaign, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

The 2024 title is real, and it matters. But it's also the one season that breaks the pattern: the year Day handed play-calling to an experienced, independent coordinator (Chip Kelly) rather than controlling it himself or delegating to someone without full authority. Every other year, including the very next season, the same collapse pattern returned. A single championship doesn't erase seven years of documented late-game failures against elite competition, especially when the title year is the outlier, not the norm, in that dataset.

Q. His overall record is 85-15. That's one of the best in the country.

True, and worth stating plainly: Ohio State wins almost everything against everyone except the best teams. That's exactly the problem. A strong overall record against a mostly favorable schedule, paired with a 33% win rate against top-five teams and a 7-8 mark in one-score games, tells you the losses aren't randomly distributed: they cluster specifically in the games that determine championships. Volume of wins against weaker competition doesn't offset a consistent inability to close out the games that matter most.

Q. He's one of the best recruiters in the country. Doesn't that count for something?

Yes, genuinely. Day's recruiting has kept Ohio State stocked with elite talent, including multiple first-round NFL picks at wide receiver alone. But that's precisely what makes the losing pattern harder to excuse, not easier. This isn't a talent problem. Ohio State has entered nearly every one of these losses as the more talented team. Elite recruiting raises the ceiling; it doesn't explain why the team can't hold a 20-point lead against it.

Q. Every big program loses close games sometimes. You're cherry-picking a narrative.

Fair pressure-test, and here's the actual data rather than just a narrative: 7 of 15 losses specifically involved blowing a lead. That's 47%, not an occasional bad night. Compare that to Urban Meyer's tenure, where this specific failure mode was rare. The frequency and consistency of this exact pattern, across four different play-callers and play-calling structures, is what separates this from normal college football variance.

Q. Coaching changes are disruptive and often make things worse. Look at programs that regretted firing a good coach.

This is a legitimate risk, and it's worth Ohio State's administration weighing carefully. There's no guarantee a new coach performs better, and coaching transitions can set a program back a cycle or two. Our position isn't that a change is risk-free. It's that a documented, seven-year pattern at the sport's highest level, from a program with the resources to demand better, warrants action rather than running back the same structure again. Our position is that Ohio State should move on from Ryan Day, and that at the very least the staff he hired should go. The status quo has earned more than scrutiny.

Q. Arthur Smith just got hired. Isn't it too early to judge him?

A handful of games isn't a fair full sample, and we're not claiming otherwise. But his hiring itself, someone with a 21-30 NFL head coaching record, no college experience, and two below-average years as an NFL coordinator, was a decision that could be evaluated before a single snap was played, based entirely on his existing track record. Since Texas, Ohio State has also lost at USC (30–18, never led) and to Oregon (41–28, after leading 14–0). The Texas collapse alone doesn't prove Smith will fail long-term. It does raise the question of whether his résumé was ever the right fit for fixing this specific, pre-existing problem.

We're not arguing Ryan Day is untalented.

We're arguing that a specific, recurring, well-documented pattern, seven years running, regardless of who calls the plays, deserves action rather than being written off year after year as bad luck. Reasonable people can disagree on how far to go. The data says the status quo can't stand, and at the very least the staff has to change.
